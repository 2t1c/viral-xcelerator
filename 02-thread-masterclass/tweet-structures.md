# Writing Each Tweet (Reusable Tweet Structure)

Once your outline is locked, every tweet becomes a simple question of structure. You're not inventing each tweet from scratch — you're picking the right structure for the point, then filling it in like a template.

Students who get fluent at this can write a full 7-tweet thread in 30 minutes. The structures do most of the thinking for them.

## Fill It Like a Placeholder

Think of each structure as a fill-in-the-blank. Drop your idea into a proven skeleton, then refine over a few passes.

Treat it as a spiral, not a straight line: write rough, then each pass either sharpens a line, adds a missing detail, or reorders for flow. By the second or third pass, most tweets are solid.

## The Core Structures

### 1. What-Why-How
The most versatile. Works for nearly any educational or insight tweet.
- **What:** the main idea · **Why:** why it matters · **How:** the step or application

> Most people read to finish books, not to remember them.
>
> That's why you forget 90% of what you read within a week.
>
> Fix it: after each chapter, close the book and write one sentence on what mattered.

### 2. What-How
Stripped-down. Use when the "why" is obvious or already covered.

> Cold emails die in the first line.
>
> Open with one specific detail about them, not about you.

### 3. What-Why
Use when there's no clear action step, or the insight itself is the value.

> Compound interest feels slow because it's invisible early.
>
> The first 10 years build almost nothing. The next 10 build everything.

### 4. Problem-Solution
The standard for any tweet that names a pain point.

> You're not bad at focusing. Your phone is just built to win.
>
> Leave it in another room. Willpower can't beat a slot machine in your pocket.

### 5. Before-After
Transformation framing. Powerful when there's a clear delta.

> Two years ago I couldn't write 100 words without freezing.
>
> Now I publish daily and barely think about it. One habit did that.

### 6. The Listicle
One of the highest-performing formats online. Use it with 3+ parallel items, each short enough to stand on its own line.

> 3 books that rewired how I think:
>
> - Thinking, Fast and Slow — how your brain fools you
> - Atomic Habits — why systems beat goals
> - Meditations — how to stay calm when everything isn't

## End Every Tweet on an Open Loop

A thread only works if the reader keeps going. The last line of each tweet should pull them into the next.

- Hint at something without delivering it.
- Start a thought but hold the payoff.
- Tease the turn that's coming.

> "But the move that changed everything came next…"
> "That's when his plan backfired…"

## Don't Squeeze a Tweet

If an idea is too big for 280 characters, split it into two. Split when:
- The idea has two beats that lose impact crammed together
- You're cutting important context to make it fit
- The tweet feels rushed even after editing
- A cliffhanger sits naturally between the halves

A two-tweet point often hits harder — the split creates an open loop.

## Requirements For Every Tweet

- **Easy to read.** A wall of text loses the reader.
- **One sentence per line.** Let each idea breathe.
- **Double spacing.** Blank line between sentences so it's clean when pasted into X.
- **Bullets for 3+ items.** Order by length or importance.
- **Specific numbers.** "15," not "a teenager." "$46M," not "a lot."
- **6th-grade language.** Simple words, short sentences. Hemingway style.
- **No AI tells.** Cut *realm, delve, captivating, unwavering, revolutionize*.
- **Under 280 characters** (hard cap). **Under 230 when possible** — shorter tweets skim better and pull readers through faster.

## Practical Workflow

1. **Pick the structure that fits.** What-Why-How is the default.
2. **Fill in the template.** Rough first; jump between sections.
3. **Cut.** Aim under 230 characters. Strip every word that doesn't carry weight.
4. **Add rhythm.** Line breaks, double spacing, bullets for parallel sets.
5. **Close the loop.** Make sure the last line pulls to the next tweet.

Repeat for every tweet. Spend the most attention on your first line — if it doesn't land, the rest never gets read.
